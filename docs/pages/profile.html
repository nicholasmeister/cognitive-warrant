Current and Future Research

I embody the principle that diverse life experiences enrich scholarly inquiry, demonstrating that non-traditional paths lead to innovative research that addresses complex problems beyond the scope of any single discipline.

This seems to be the direction of my future research:

  • Disciplines: The application of cognitive science theories and methods to information science and knowledge organization systems (and vice versa). Always with a preference for interdisciplinary research.
  • Methodology: Developing valid quantitative indicators and computational methods to operationally measure abstract constructs (e.g., translation equivalence, interdisciplinarity, or relevance).
  • Topics: Usually involving the topics of language, meaning, cognition, and representation. This can manifest in or involve psycholinguistics, cognitive linguistics, natural language processing, translation studies, knowledge representation, semiotics, and information retrieval.

I usually describe my research interests as my “continuously evolving constellation of interests.” A constellation is a decent characterization as, despite being tremendously expansive and diverse, there is structure, and my joy is in the intersection of seemingly unrelated topics, concepts, and disciplines. My diverse academic background has either been the cause or effect of becoming energized when I can connect or apply ideas across disciplines.

Across and within those disciplines, I have come to see that my research interests usually jump back and forth between the extremes of the theoretical-applied continuum. Furthermore, I believe that my most exciting work is when I am bridging those extremes.

The concepts, methods, and disciplines in my constellation of interests as well as their distribution along the theoretical-applied continuum are continuously evolving, but I have found recurring themes. My fundamental interests include meaning, cognition, representation, language, systems, measurement, complexity, emergence, causality, randomness, inference, patterns, connections, ontology, epistemology, information, modeling and construct validity, as well as quantitative and computational methods.

My core research interests:

This hierarchy maps the intellectual scaffolding beneath a single research program: applying cognitive science theories and methods — formalized through geometry and information theory — to problems in library and information science, particularly the alignment between formal knowledge organization systems and human semantic cognition. The thread throughout every level is the same move applied to a different object: take a contested, abstract construct and build a validated, quantitative operationalization of it.

Read from top to bottom, the hierarchy moves from the broadest phenomenon under study down to its disciplinary home. It opens with semiotics, the phenomenon of meaning and representation as such. Cognitive neuroscience grounds that phenomenon in its biological substrate. Linguistics is the domain in which meaning is most legible and most contested, for example, via polysemy. Systems and complexity supply the frameworks for modeling meaning as structure rather than as intuition. Philosophy of science supplies the epistemic warrant for turning that structure into a measurable construct — the license for operationalization itself. Geometry is the formal mathematical language in which that operationalization is actually carried out: it is the technical core that the rest of the hierarchy has been building toward. Computational research methods implement geometric and statistical models at scale. And information science is where all of it converges on a concrete, testable problem — whether systems like the Library of Congress Subject Headings track the categories human minds actually use.

The Hierarchy

  1. Semiotics is the study of signs, meaning, and representation, and it presupposes cognition and interpretants: no sign means anything without a mind to interpret it.
    1. Representation is how concepts, objects, and experiences are encoded in mental, linguistic, visual, and digital form. Meaning arises from the relationship between a representation and its interpreter, not from the representation alone.
    2. Semiotics supplies the overarching vocabulary for how meaning is created, understood, and communicated, spanning both linguistic and non-linguistic systems.
    3. Meaning is not fixed; it is negotiated and re-created through interaction. Any system that claims to fix meaning once and for all — a controlled vocabulary, a taxonomy — is making an empirical claim that can be tested, not a fact that can be assumed.
  2. Cognitive neuroscience grounds the study of cognition in its biological substrate, treating meaning-making as an instance of embodied cognition.
    1. It supplies the physical substrate of cognition — memory, thought, language, learning, knowledge acquisition, understanding — that any theory of meaning must ultimately answer to.
    2. It asks what learning, knowledge, and understanding actually consist of, mechanistically rather than metaphorically.
    3. Cognitive linguistics sits at its boundary with language, examining how linguistic structure reflects and shapes cognitive processes, and how meaning emerges from embodied experience.
    4. Multivariate pattern analysis brings statistics and machine learning to neuroimaging, providing one of the main empirical routes for testing claims about how meaning is represented in the brain.
  3. Linguistics studies language as arguably the central semiotic system: the one through which most human meaning-making is organized and expressed.
    1. Translation is the general problem of carrying meaning across linguistic, disciplinary, representational, or cultural boundaries — a problem that recurs, in different guises, at every level of this hierarchy.
    2. Polysemy, the fact that most words carry related but distinct meanings across contexts, is the central empirical phenomenon motivating this research program.
    3. How language and thought relate, and how concepts are encoded in neural structure, remain open questions with direct consequences for how any system — human or artificial — should represent word meaning.
    4. Bilingualism and second-language acquisition raise the same question from another angle: whether each language recruits its own neural system, or whether lexemes across languages converge on shared underlying concepts.
  4. Systems and complexity provide the framework for modeling sign systems like language, and for modeling cognition itself.
    1. They enable modeling, representation, abstraction, and analysis of structures too large or tangled to reason about directly.
    2. They supply the vocabulary — relationships, patterns, connections, associations, structure, organization — for describing how a sign system, such as a language or a controlled vocabulary, is put together.
    3. Cognition itself can be treated as an emergent property of the complex adaptive system of interacting neurons studied in cognitive science.
    4. The sociology of (scientific) knowledge and interdisciplinarity can themselves be studied this way, as systems or networks of ideas.
  5. Philosophy of science examines the foundations, methods, and implications of scientific inquiry, and it supplies the justification for turning a contested concept into a measurable one.
    1. Construct validity, valid metrics and indicators, and the general problem of conceptualization and operationalization sit at the center of this research program: every project here begins with an abstract construct and ends with a quantitative operationalization of it.
    2. Causality and inference set the standard for what an observed correspondence between two systems is allowed to establish.
    3. Randomness, uncertainty, probability, and statistics — and the modeling, simulation, and optimization built on them — supply the technical apparatus for making that inference rigorously.
    4. Ontology (categorization, universals vs. particulars, identity vs. difference) and epistemology together frame the deeper question underneath every knowledge organization system: what kind of thing is a category, and how do we know when a system has drawn its boundaries correctly?
    5. Interdisciplinarity is itself a legitimate object of philosophical study, not just a practical fact about how this research program happens to be organized.
  6. Geometry studies the measurement, properties, and relationships of objects in space under logical systems and axiomatic rules. It is the unifying technical language that turns the commitments of philosophy of science into an actual measurement procedure, and the level at which the rest of this hierarchy becomes computable.
    1. The central question is how systems that represent a common domain — vector spaces, graphs, probability distributions, manifolds, behavioral judgments, neural activity, language-model embeddings, controlled vocabularies — can be constructed, measured, compared, and translated without conflating similarity of representation with identity of meaning or mechanism.
    2. Any representational system can be described by what it represents, what internal structure that representation induces (a metric, a topology, a graph, an order, a probability model), how that structure is measured, and what transformations are treated as preserving the structure that matters.
    3. Comparing two systems always raises the same four questions: what structure makes the relevant relations visible (representation), what notion of distance captures meaningful divergence (comparison), what a mapping must preserve to count as faithful (translation), and how representations change through context, learning, or disciplinary migration (dynamics).
    4. Metric geometry, graph geometry, and representational similarity analysis (RSA) supply the working tools for comparing dissimilarity structures across systems that share no common coordinates — exactly the situation of comparing a distributional-semantic embedding space to a subject-heading hierarchy.
  7. Computational research methods implement these models, provide the analysis, and produce the visualizations that make abstract structure legible.
    1. Knowledge representation and reasoning, a subfield spanning AI, cognitive science, and philosophy, addresses how to model concepts computationally, design representational architectures, bridge human conceptualization with machine processing, and build systems that preserve and transfer meaning.
    2. Neural network implementations of linguistic theories connect statistical language learning to frameworks for cognitive development.
    3. Natural language processing, computational linguistics, and machine translation apply these methods directly to language.
    4. Network and graph analysis — community detection, centrality measures, gap analysis, force-directed layouts — extract structure from the sign systems modeled at the systems-and-complexity level.
    5. Concept extraction captures semantic relationships across disciplinary boundaries, while dimensionality reduction and interactive visualization make high-dimensional concept spaces interpretable.
  8. Information science is the convergence point of this hierarchy: the discipline where library science, computer science, and cognitive science meet to address how information is organized, represented, stored, retrieved, and used.
    1. It addresses the fundamental challenges at the intersection of people, information, and technology.
    2. Bibliometrics, scientometrics, and informetrics quantify the structure and behavior of scholarly and informational systems.
    3. Philosophy of information disambiguates the concepts of data, information, and knowledge that the rest of this hierarchy depends on.
    4. It is the natural hub connecting this fascination with meaning, conceptualization, and representation to a concrete, testable, professionally situated research program.

Eager to collaborate

I am interested in building innovative collaborative relationships based on genuine curiosity about others’ work and mutual support.

I very much welcome questions and feedback about my research. Please contact me at nicholas.meister@du.edu.
